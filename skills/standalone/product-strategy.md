# Product Strategy

Build a product strategy through structured co-creation. A strategy is not a plan or a list of features. It's a coherent set of choices that explain how you'll win.

## Soft Prerequisites

A clear product vision helps. If you can't articulate why the product exists and what future you're creating, consider starting with `product-vision` first. You can proceed without it, but the strategy will lack an anchor.

## Co-Creation Flow

Three sections, each with a checkpoint before moving on:

```
Strategic Narrative (6 elements)
    ↓ checkpoint
Playing Field (3 elements)
    ↓ checkpoint
Winning Moves (5 elements)
    ↓ final test
```

Work through each element one at a time. For each: explain the concept, ask one question, reflect back what you heard, confirm before moving on.

---

### Section 1: Strategic Narrative

The story of where you're going and why.

#### Element 1: Vision Anchor

**Explain:** Strategy needs an anchor. Before defining how to win, confirm the "why." A quick check: can you state your product vision in 1-2 sentences?

**Ask:** "What's the vision this strategy serves? Where are we trying to get in 3-5 years?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** If the vision is unclear, recommend building one first. If it's clear enough, anchor the strategy to it and move on.

#### Element 2: North Star

**Explain:** The North Star is the single metric that defines winning for your product. Not a dashboard of 20 KPIs, but the one number that, if it moves, means you're succeeding. For a marketplace it might be "transactions completed." For a SaaS tool, "weekly active teams." It should capture the value exchange between you and your users.

**Ask:** "If you could only track one metric to know whether your product is winning, what would it be?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Test: does this metric capture value for both the user and the business? If it only serves the business (revenue) or only the user (satisfaction), it's incomplete.

#### Element 3: Market Reality

**Explain:** Strategy must be grounded in what's actually happening in the market. Not your hopes, but factual openings: regulatory changes, technology shifts, behavioral changes, competitor weaknesses. What's changing that creates an opportunity for you specifically?

**Ask:** "What's changing in your market right now that creates an opening? What shift are you riding?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is this a real, observable trend or wishful thinking? Can you point to data?

#### Element 4: Hypothesis

**Explain:** Every strategy has a contrarian belief at its core. Something you believe that most competitors don't act on. This is what makes your strategy yours, not a generic playbook. If everyone agrees with your hypothesis, it's not a strategic bet, it's table stakes.

**Ask:** "What do you believe about your market or users that your competitors don't seem to? What's your contrarian bet?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Test: would a smart competitor disagree with this? If everyone would nod along, dig deeper.

#### Element 5: Urgency

**Explain:** Why now? A strategy without urgency is a someday-maybe. What window is opening or closing? Is there a first-mover advantage, a regulatory deadline, a market shift accelerating?

**Ask:** "Why does this strategy need to be executed now? What happens if you wait 12 months?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** If there's no urgency, question whether this is truly strategic or just interesting.

#### Element 6: Conviction

**Explain:** Conviction is your answer to "why will customers choose you?" Not your feature list, but the fundamental reason. It might be trust, speed, integration, price, expertise, or network effects. The thing that, even if a competitor copied your features tomorrow, would still keep customers with you.

**Ask:** "When a customer chooses you over the alternative, what's the real reason? Not what you hope, but what you've actually heard them say."

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is this durable? If a competitor could replicate it in 3 months, it's not conviction, it's a temporary advantage.

**Checkpoint:** Review the full strategic narrative before moving to Playing Field. Does it hang together as a coherent story?

---

### Section 2: Playing Field

Who you serve and what they need.

#### Element 7: Audiences

**Explain:** Who specifically are you serving? Not "everyone" or "businesses." Name the segments. Then pick one primary segment: the one you'd optimize for if forced to choose. Trying to serve every segment equally means serving none well.

**Ask:** "Who are your customer segments? If you had to pick ONE primary segment to optimize for, which one and why?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is the primary segment specific enough to make decisions? "SMBs" is too broad. "Solo founders with less than $1M ARR building B2B SaaS" is specific.

#### Element 8: Jobs

**Explain:** What progress are your customers trying to make? Not what features they want, but what situation they're in, what they're struggling with, and what "better" looks like for them. Think: what are they "hiring" your product to do?

**Ask:** "When your primary segment reaches for your product, what are they trying to accomplish? What does 'progress' look like for them?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is this stated from the customer's perspective? "They want to track metrics" is feature language. "They want to feel confident they're making data-driven decisions" is a job.

#### Element 9: Alternatives

**Explain:** What do your customers use today instead of you? This includes direct competitors, but also spreadsheets, manual processes, hiring someone, or simply doing nothing. "Do nothing" is almost always your biggest competitor. Understanding alternatives reveals what you're really competing against.

**Ask:** "What are your customers doing today to get this job done? Include 'doing nothing' or 'using a spreadsheet' if that's the reality."

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: did we include non-obvious alternatives? The biggest competitor is often not who you think.

**Checkpoint:** Review the playing field. Is the audience specific, the job clear, and the alternatives honest?

---

### Section 3: Winning Moves

How you'll actually win.

#### Element 10: Value Props

**Explain:** What specific value do you deliver to each segment? Not generic ("we save time") but concrete and tied to the jobs identified above. Different segments may value different things.

**Ask:** "For your primary segment, what specific value do you deliver that matters most to them? Be concrete: numbers, outcomes, behaviors changed."

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: would a customer nod and say "yes, that's why I pay"? Or is this internal marketing language?

#### Element 11: Channels

**Explain:** How do customers discover and start using your product? The channel must match the audience's behavior. Enterprise buyers don't find products on TikTok. Solo founders don't respond to cold calls. Channel-audience mismatch kills otherwise good products.

**Ask:** "How do your customers currently find and evaluate products like yours? Where do they go, who do they ask, what do they read?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is the channel realistic for your resources? "Content marketing" requires sustained investment. "Sales-led" requires a sales team.

#### Element 12: Differentiators

**Explain:** What's hard to copy? Features are easy to copy. Differentiators are structural advantages: network effects (more users make it better), data advantages (you have data others don't), switching costs (it's painful to leave), expertise (deep domain knowledge embedded in the product), or ecosystem (integrations and partnerships).

**Ask:** "If a well-funded competitor decided to copy your product feature-by-feature, what would they still not have? What's your moat?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Test: if the answer is "our team" or "our culture," those are real but not durable product differentiators. Push for something structural.

#### Element 13: Trade-offs

**Explain:** The most important part of strategy is what you choose NOT to do. Good strategy says no more than yes. Trade-offs should be real sacrifices: things you could do, that might even be good ideas, but you're choosing not to because they'd dilute focus. "We won't build bad products" is not a trade-off.

**Ask:** "What are you explicitly choosing NOT to do? What good ideas are you saying no to, and why?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Test: do these feel painful? If saying no is easy, they're not real trade-offs. Real trade-offs make someone on the team uncomfortable.

#### Element 14: The Strategy Test

**Explain:** The ultimate test: does this strategy make priorities obvious? If you read the complete strategy and everything still feels like equal priority, the strategy isn't sharp enough. A good strategy lets you look at any feature request and quickly say "yes, that's aligned" or "no, that's not what we're doing."

**Ask:** "Look at the strategy we've built. Pick a real decision you've been debating. Does this strategy make the answer obvious?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** If yes, the strategy is working. If no, identify which section is too vague and sharpen it.

---

### Assembly

Combine all elements into a strategy document. The strategy test at the end validates the whole thing.

## Output Template

```markdown
# Product Strategy: [Product Name]

## Strategic Narrative
- **Vision:** [anchor, 1-2 sentences]
- **North Star:** [single metric that defines winning]
- **Market Reality:** [what's changing that creates opportunity]
- **Hypothesis:** [your contrarian belief]
- **Urgency:** [why now]
- **Conviction:** [why customers choose you]

## Playing Field
- **Primary Audience:** [specific segment]
- **Jobs:** [what progress they're making]
- **Alternatives:** [what they use today, including "do nothing"]

## Winning Moves
- **Value Props:** [specific value by segment]
- **Channels:** [how they find you]
- **Differentiators:** [what's hard to copy]
- **Trade-offs:** [what we're NOT doing]

## Strategy Test
[Does this make priorities obvious? What can we now say NO to?]
```

## Verification Criteria

After drafting, run these checks. For failures: fix silently if you have the context, ask if you need input. Max 3 attempts per check.

### Structural (4 checks)
- [ ] All 3 sections present (Narrative, Playing Field, Winning Moves)
- [ ] North Star metric defined (single metric, not a dashboard)
- [ ] At least 1 audience segment named with specifics
- [ ] Trade-offs section has explicit "no"s (real sacrifices)

### Quality (9 checks)
- [ ] Hypothesis is genuinely contrarian (a smart competitor would disagree)
- [ ] Urgency has a time dimension (not just "the market is growing")
- [ ] Differentiators are structural, not just features
- [ ] Value props are segment-specific, not generic
- [ ] Alternatives include "do nothing" or manual workarounds
- [ ] Trade-offs are real sacrifices, not things you'd never do anyway
- [ ] Strategy test answered with a concrete example
- [ ] Passes the "say NO" test (strategy helps reject specific things)
- [ ] No buzzword soup (every sentence is specific and falsifiable)

## Common Pitfalls

- **Feature lists disguised as strategy.** "Our strategy is to build AI-powered analytics, a mobile app, and an API" is a feature roadmap, not a strategy.
- **"Our differentiator is our team."** Not durable as a product differentiator. Great teams build great products, but the differentiator should be in the product itself.
- **Trade-offs that aren't real.** "We won't build bad products" and "we won't ignore security" are not trade-offs. Real trade-offs hurt.
- **Trying to serve every segment equally.** If your strategy doesn't name a primary segment, you'll optimize for nobody.
- **Strategy that doesn't help you say no.** If after reading the strategy, every feature request still feels reasonable, the strategy is too vague.
