# Product Alignment One-Pager

Build a Product Alignment Document through structured co-creation. A PAD evolves through two phases: Opportunity Framing (is this worth solving?) and Solution Framing (how should we solve it?). It ends with a clear decision request.

Great one-pagers communicate the data, insights, and beliefs behind a bet. The goal is not to manufacture certainty but to take a data-informed perspective on risk and return.

## Soft Prerequisites

A PAD works best when strategy and metrics exist. If you don't know what outcomes matter, the initiative might solve a real problem that doesn't move the needle. Consider `product-strategy` and `product-okrs` first if strategic context is missing.

## Co-Creation Flow

Three phases, each with a checkpoint:

```
Phase 1: Opportunity Framing (6 elements)
    ↓ checkpoint: is this worth framing a solution for?
Phase 2: Solution Framing (4 elements)
    ↓ checkpoint: review solution framing
Phase 3: Expected Outcome (2 elements)
    ↓ assemble
```

Work through each element one at a time. For each: explain the concept, ask one question, reflect back what you heard, confirm before moving on.

---

### Phase 1: Opportunity Framing

Define the problem without diving into solutions. Explore all possible directions.

#### Element 1: Problem Statement

**Explain:** What problem are we solving, or what opportunity are we addressing? State it as a problem, not a solution. Keep features out of the title and framing. "Add tags to work orders" is a feature. "Help 7,000 coordinators process work orders 50% faster" is an opportunity.

**Ask:** "What problem are you solving or opportunity are you pursuing? Describe it without mentioning any specific solution or feature."

**Reflect/Confirm:** Apply the test: does it describe a user need or a feature? Rewrite if needed.

#### Element 2: Audience

**Explain:** For whom are we solving this? Be specific: role, segment, size, characteristics. "Our users" is not an audience. "In-house maintenance coordinators at companies with 30+ seat accounts" is.

**Ask:** "Who specifically has this problem? Can you describe the audience with enough detail that we could identify them in your data?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: could you query a database or run a filter to find these people? If not, get more specific.

#### Element 3: Rationale

**Explain:** How do we know this is the right problem to solve right now? This is where you make the case. Connect to OKRs or strategy. Link to customer research, conversations, data. Consider: will this neutralize competition, differentiate your product, or delight users? And the key question: why now? What happens if you wait 12 months?

**Ask:** "Why is this the most important problem to solve right now? What evidence supports it, and what happens if you do nothing?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is the evidence concrete (data, quotes, observations) or assumed? Is the urgency real?

#### Element 4: Success Metrics

**Explain:** How do we know if we solved this problem? Not "we shipped it" but measurable impact. Think behavioral change: what will users do more of, less of, start doing, stop doing? Define baseline and target.

**Ask:** "If this initiative succeeds, what measurable change do you expect? What's the metric, where is it today, and where should it be?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is this an outcome metric (retention, activation) or a vanity metric (pageviews)? Does it connect to the OKRs referenced in the rationale?

#### Element 5: Competitive Research

**Explain:** How did others capture this opportunity or solve this problem? This includes direct competitors, adjacent products, and non-obvious approaches. Understanding how others approached it reveals proven patterns and gaps you can exploit.

**Ask:** "How have others, competitors, adjacent products, or even non-tech solutions, addressed this problem? What worked and what didn't?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: did we look beyond direct competitors? Sometimes the best insight comes from a completely different industry solving the same underlying job.

#### Element 6: Solution Directions

**Explain:** Explore 2-3 high-level solution directions. Not detailed specs, but different approaches to solving the problem. Short descriptions or sketches. Compare them roughly: impact vs complexity using t-shirt sizing (days, weeks, months). Don't fall in love with the first idea. A diverse cross-functional team might find an even better solution to a compelling problem.

**Ask:** "What are 2-3 different ways you could approach this problem? Think broadly: different scopes, different entry points, different levels of investment."

**Reflect/Confirm:** Map each direction with rough impact and complexity. Check: are these genuinely different approaches, or one idea with variations?

**Checkpoint:** Is the opportunity compelling enough to frame a solution? Review problem, audience, rationale, metrics, and directions before committing to a solution.

---

### Phase 2: Solution Framing

Select the most viable direction and define the solution.

#### Element 7: Proposed Solution

**Explain:** What's the scope of the selected solution? Key capabilities (not a feature spec). How difficult will it be to realize? Challenge yourself: can you cut scope by 75% and still deliver value? If 80% of the value comes from 20% of the work, start there.

**Ask:** "Which direction from Phase 1 are you leaning toward? What's the minimum scope that delivers meaningful value?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is this the minimum viable scope, or did scope creep back in? What could you cut and still learn?

#### Element 8: Why Alternatives Were Rejected

**Explain:** Explain why the other directions weren't chosen. This builds stakeholder trust and prevents the "why didn't you consider X?" question. Include links to research, data, or user feedback that informed the choice.

**Ask:** "For the directions you didn't choose: why not? What made the selected approach better?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: are the reasons substantive? "Felt too complex" is weak. "Required 3x the engineering time with similar projected impact" is strong.

#### Element 9: Out of Scope

**Explain:** What is explicitly NOT included? Now vs Later boundaries. Saying what you won't do is as important as saying what you will. It prevents scope creep and sets stakeholder expectations. Don't split artificially into phases: each scope should be independently valuable.

**Ask:** "What are you explicitly deferring to 'later'? What are you explicitly NOT doing at all? Is the 'now' scope independently valuable without the 'later' work?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is "now" independently valuable? If it only creates value when "later" ships too, the scope isn't right.

#### Element 10: Dependencies, Risks & Mitigations

**Explain:** What could go wrong, and what do we depend on? Map dependencies on other teams or systems. Assess the four risks (value, usability, feasibility, business viability). Then a pre-mortem: imagine it's 6 months from now and this initiative failed. What are 3 plausible reasons? Pre-mortems surface risks that optimism hides.

**Ask:** "What does this depend on (teams, systems, timelines)? And the pre-mortem: it's 6 months from now and this failed. Tell me 3 plausible stories of why."

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: are the pre-mortem scenarios honest (internal failures, wrong assumptions) or only external blame (market shifted, team was pulled away)?

**Checkpoint:** Review the full solution framing before moving to the decision.

---

### Phase 3: Expected Outcome

#### Element 11: GTM Strategy

**Explain:** How will this reach users? Release plan and key milestones (alpha, beta, GA). What marketing, support, or operational preparation is needed? Any pricing or packaging implications? This ensures the initiative doesn't ship into a void.

**Ask:** "What's the release plan? What needs to happen beyond engineering for this to succeed?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Check: is there a plan for adoption, not just delivery? Shipping is not the finish line.

#### Element 12: Decision Request

**Explain:** Every PAD ends with a clear ask. You're not presenting "for discussion," you're requesting a decision. Four possible outcomes. Stakeholders should know exactly what you're asking them to decide.

**Ask:** "What decision are you requesting? Is this ready for a full 'go,' or are you seeking directional approval with a followup checkpoint?"

**Reflect/Confirm:** Confirm which of the four outcomes is most likely, and prepare for the conversation.

---

### Assembly

Combine all elements into a Product Alignment One-Pager. Apply a final gut-check: is it collaborative, independently valuable, outcome-oriented, understandable, actionable, humble (surfaces unknowns), succinct, and testable?

## Verification Criteria

After drafting, run these checks. For failures: fix silently if you have the context, ask if you need input. Max 3 attempts per check.

### Structural (5 checks)
- [ ] Problem stated as a problem, not a solution
- [ ] Audience is specific (not "our users")
- [ ] Rationale links to OKRs or strategy with evidence
- [ ] At least 2 genuinely different solution directions explored
- [ ] Decision request present with 4 outcome options

### Quality (8 checks)
- [ ] Success metrics have baseline and target
- [ ] Solution directions are genuinely different (not one idea with variations)
- [ ] Rejected alternatives have substantive reasons (not strawmen)
- [ ] "Now" scope is independently valuable without "Later"
- [ ] Scope was challenged for reduction (75% cut test considered)
- [ ] Pre-mortem has 3 plausible scenarios including internal failures
- [ ] One-pager covers one problem (independently valuable, not a bundle)
- [ ] GTM plan exists beyond just "ship it"

## Common Pitfalls

- **Jumping to Phase 2 without genuinely exploring alternatives.** If you explored 3 directions but had already decided on one before Phase 1, the exploration is theatre.
- **"Competitive research: no one does this."** Almost never true. Someone is solving some version of this problem somehow. Look harder, look at adjacent industries.
- **Scope that keeps everything in "Now."** If nothing is deferred, you haven't made trade-offs. Challenge every element: does it need to be in v1?
- **Pre-mortem that only blames externals.** "The market shifted" and "we got deprioritized" are real but lazy. What internal assumptions could be wrong? What if users don't adopt?
- **Feature-titled initiatives.** "Build recommendation engine" tells stakeholders nothing. "Help power users discover relevant content, reducing time-to-value by 50%" tells them why it matters.
